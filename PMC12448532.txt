Dear Editor, Ayurveda curriculum and thus education system has changed its structure and function drastically in the last two years as per NCISM directives. Various novel teaching learning methods have been adopted in the curriculum. One of the very effective T-L methods to improve the clinical skills is a standardized patients program. Standardized patients (SPs)  are trained individuals who simulate real-life health conditions and patient interactions to help students or practitioners practice their clinical skills in a controlled and educational setting.[ 1 ] This technique is widely used in medical education and has also found potential to teach the holistic diagnostic approach, patient care, and treatment strategies from an Ayurvedic perspective. Here is an overview of the SP protocol for Ayurveda. Purpose of standardized patients in Ayurveda 
 
 Clinical Training:  To provide Ayurvedic students and practitioners with realistic, hands-on experience in assessing, diagnosing, and treating patients. Holistic Diagnosis:  The SP approach emphasizes Ayurvedic diagnostic methods, such as pulse diagnosis ( Nadi Pariksha ), tongue examination ( Jihva Pariksha ), urine examination ( Mutra Pariksha ), and other observational techniques ( Darshan, Sparshan, Prashna ). Communication Skills:  SPs help develop effective doctor–patient communication skills, which are essential in Ayurveda, where empathy and understanding are critical. Evaluating Practical Skills of Students:  SPs serve as a controlled tool for assessing the practitioner’s competency in diagnosing and prescribing Ayurvedic therapies (such as herbal medicine, Panchakarma, dietary advice, etc.). Case Scenarios These cases might include common ailments, such as digestive issues, chronic fatigue, skin disorders, stress-related disorders, or more complex multisystem disorders. Each case is designed to challenge the student in different areas of Ayurvedic practice (diagnosis, treatment, and patient education). Simulating Symptoms SPs are trained to exhibit the symptoms and behaviors associated with specific Ayurvedic imbalances. For example, a Pitta-type SP might have symptoms of anger, inflammation, acidity, etc., while a Vata-type SP might exhibit signs of dryness, restlessness, and digestive issues. Prakriti Parikshan The assessment of Prakriti can be performed on SPs very well. Questions can directly be asked to the SPs and answers can be noted; hence, Prakriti of SPs can be assessed. Patient Interaction Initial Consultation: The Ayurvedic practitioner will begin the consultation by taking a comprehensive health history, focusing on the mind-body connection and the patient’s lifestyle (diet, sleep patterns, exercise, emotional stress, etc.). Observation and examination Pulse Diagnosis (Nadi Pariksha): The practitioner uses pulse diagnosis as part of the examination. The SP will simulate a specific pulse type that matches their assigned dosha. Tongue and Urine Examination: The SP may simulate a tongue coating or color that corresponds to a doshic imbalance or show urine color changes based on diet or health status. Touch and Visual Observation (Sparshan and Darshan): The SP might act out signs like skin temperature, texture, or other body features that may reflect their condition Treatment and Follow-Up Based on the history taken by the learner, he/she can prepare the investigations and treatment plan, but the SPs should not be intervened by the treatment as they are dummy patients in one since. Follow-Up: SPs may return for follow-up consultations, allowing the Ayurvedic practitioner to assess treatment efficacy and adjust as needed. AETCOM Attitude, Ethics, and Communication Standardization of the SP experience 
 
 Training:  Standardized patients undergo rigorous training to ensure consistency in how they portray their symptoms and respond to questions. They are coached on how to act in a way that mimics real-life conditions accurately. Scenarios:  Each SP is given a standardized script to follow, ensuring that every practitioner sees the same symptoms and history when interacting with the SP. The aim is to provide an identical experience to each practitioner or student. Scoring Rubric:  Learners are evaluated on their ability to perform an Ayurvedic consultation. The rubric may include: Comprehensive health history and assessment. Adequate use of Ayurvedic diagnostic methods. Proper treatment recommendations (herbs, diet, Panchakarma). Communication skills and empathy. Cultural competence, respecting Ayurvedic principles and patient concerns. Attitude, Ethics, and Communication (AETCOM) Challenges and considerations 
 
 Training of SPs:  Training in cultural competence is essential. Therapeutic Approach:  Ayurveda emphasizes a personalized approach to treatment, so the SP must respond in ways that reflect the diverse cultural backgrounds of patients. Compensations:  SPs devote their time for a noble cause, and hence, they deserve financial compensation for their work. Benefits of SP program in Ayurveda 
 
 Practical Experience:  SPs provide learners with a safe, low-risk environment in which they can practice diagnostics and treatments. Feedback and Reflection:  SPs provide feedback after the consultation, giving the learner a chance to reflect on their performance and improve. Development of Clinical Competence:  SPs provide an opportunity to practice complex diagnostic and treatment techniques, which are crucial in Ayurveda. Ethical considerations 
 
 Confidentiality:  As with any medical practice, confidentiality must be upheld. SPs are given scripts that do not involve real personal data, but their privacy must still be respected. Respect for the Patient Role:  The SP must feel respected in their role and not as a “test subject.” They should not feel pressured into portraying any symptom or result in a specific way.[ 2 ] Novelty of topic This study presents a standardized patient program as a crucial innovation in Ayurvedic education. It offers a risk-free, interactive learning environment that allows learners to improve their clinical skills and decision-making abilities by engaging with a variety of clinical scenarios, including rare and complex cases. By integrating modern educational technologies, this approach complements traditional Ayurvedic training and better equips learners to address the evolving challenges of health care. The health promotion component of a standardized patients (SP) program aims to equip healthcare professionals with the skills to engage patients effectively in preventive care, healthy lifestyle choices, and early diagnosis and intervention.[ 3 ] Through these role-playing exercises, students enhance their abilities in motivational interviewing, cultural competence, positive behavioral changes, non-interventional clinical examinations, and health education, fostering patient autonomy and self-management. Additionally, SPs enable providers to tailor health messages for diverse populations, assess health literacy, and promote preventive screenings, supporting a patient-centered approach to long-term health improvement contributing to overall community well-being. Conclusion The use of standardized patients in Ayurveda is a vital educational tool for training Ayurveda practitioners in holistic diagnosis and treatment. By simulating real-life clinical situations, SPs provide a controlled and effective environment for learners to develop their skills in Ayurveda. This protocol helps bridge the gap between theoretical learning and real-world patient interactions, ensuring that Ayurveda practitioners are well-prepared to provide compassionate and effective care.
